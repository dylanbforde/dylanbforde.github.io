The Preschool Arms Race and the Algorithm of Ambition

Suppose two search algorithms are looking for treasure in a vast, foggy landscape. Breadth-First Search (BFS) sends hundreds of drones across the surface, scanning every inch and mapping what they can see. Depth-First Search (DFS) sends one probe straight down. It might find a hidden vein of gold, or it might fail completely.

Make the landscape the state space of possible human innovation, and the drones children. The programmers are tiger moms: parents heavily invested in optimizing their children's trajectories. They choose BFS every time.

BFS dominates when parents correlate their strategies, all pushing their children towards the same SAT-optimized, resume-padding, YC class, 'safe' milestones. That creates a pseudo-tragedy of the commons. For individual parents, chasing visible, low-risk gains is rational. Taken together, those choices leave us short of the deep, weird, uncorrelated innovations that require DFS solutions.

BFS Parenting: How Tiger Moms Colonize the Local Maxima

If you've seen a kindergarten resume listing Mandarin, violin, and quantum physics taught through nice books for children, you've seen BFS parenting. The activities follow a familiar pattern:

  • Checkbox Childhoods — Children should master the high-status skills that can be tested: math, coding, classical instruments
  • Risk Aversion — They should avoid 'wasting time' on niche interests such as competitive skateboarding or historical whaling trivia
  • Orthogonal Bragworthiness — Their activities should be diverse enough to signal "well-roundedness", but conventional enough that nobody raises an eyebrow

There's a reason parents do this. BFS works if the goal is to maximise admission to MIT or entry into quant/tech. It sends children towards known peaks in existing prestige hierarchies. Exploring unseen peaks would mean helping them find new fields instead.

So a generation climbs the same hills, getting slightly faster and slightly more impressive while the landscape's real mountains remain distant, invisible, and untouched. The children compete to drop out of Stanford and get VC funding for a GPT wrapper. Their ambitions gather around FTX and the leet code grind, while whole parts of the landscape stay undeveloped.

DFS and the Economics of Weirdness

Depth-First Search belongs to the obsessive, the eccentric, the uncorrelated. Think of the 12-year-old spending 6 hours a day writing fan fiction and reading LessWrong, or the teen turning his basement into a CRISPR lab because he thinks bioweapons are cool. There's also the autodidact learning obscure Liszt etudes when Chopin would be more useful for an exam.

DFS has high risks and high rewards. You can sink your time into a dead end (RIP, my unfinished fanfiction at 17, lol). Occasionally, though, society gets a reward out of that kind of commitment: Taleb's Black Swan innovation, as with Turing's obsession with computable numbers.

Tiger moms disincentivize DFS. Why?

  • Correlated payoffs — When all parents optimize for Ivy League admissions, they create a Nash equilibrium that punishes deviation through DFS. A kid studying AI art in 2010 was a visionary; in 2023, it's another checkbox.
  • Signalling costs — DFS projects are illegible to admissions committees until after they succeed. For every teen who "started a successful app", there are 100 who just "wasted time on computers". To me, MIT Maker Portfolios are the best example of DFS, or of strategies that go beyond talented BFS.
  • Privilege tax — DFS becomes a luxury that only the privileged, or the neglectful, can afford.

The Innovation Desert: When All Peaks Are Overcrowded

James Scott writes in Seeing Like a State about monocultures replacing diverse local ecosystems. BFS parenting does something similar to innovation: everyone is encouraged to grow the same things.

Everyone learns Python, and we get a million competent data analysts. No one learns Haskell, so the next groundbreaking functional programming paradigm goes undiscovered.

All musicians play piano or violin, so most music will be made with piano and violins. Nobody masters the theremin, and electronic music develops more slowly.

The crowded peaks also become sites of hyper-competition. Children grind LeetCode to out-code their peers, spending 90% of their effort on beating rivals rather than expanding the frontier. This is Red Queen innovation: they have to run faster just to stay in the same place.

Why They're Half Right...

"BUT BFS BUILDS FOUNDATIONAL SKILLS!!" — It does, and breadth matters. The Wright Brothers drew on physics, engineering, and bicycle mechanics. What made the difference, though, was their DFS-like obsession with flight and their willingness to give up safer careers for it.

"MOST DFS FAILS!!" — It does. Society's progress still depends on taking risks across a portfolio. Taleb describes how systems, markets, and societies need to be antifragile at the cost of fragile cells, companies, and people. If 1000 children pursuing DFS 'fail' into niche expertise, their pooled knowledge becomes new fields, new experts, or politicians who are actually good.

"TIGER MOMS PRODUCED ASIAN TECH MIRACLES!!" — Yes. These are BFS miracles, though: playing catch up through incremental optimization, rather than making paradigm shifts. China dominates AI applications but, as of right now, relies on algorithms mostly invented elsewhere.

Cultivating a Garden of Forking Paths

We still need BFS. How do we make more room for DFS alongside it?

Reward Weirdness — Colleges and employers could give more weight to narratives of obsession than to checklists. Someone who spent 3 years building a Destiny 2 discord democracy has been running a governance simulation.

Delayed Specialisation — We could follow something closer to Finland's approach, with broad curricula early and deep dives later.

Parental Bravery — Let children 'waste time' beyond what the metrics reward. Minecraft modding taught a generation to code.

The DFS Beneath BFS

The next Einstein is daydreaming on a park bench with their friends, following a thread only they can see, instead of grinding leet code problem sets. Tiger moms aren't evil. They're caught in a coordination problem: each family's sensible choice helps crowd everyone onto the same hills. Giving DFS even a little more weight might help us get beyond those local maxima.
